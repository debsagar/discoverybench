## 1. What is conserved in HPP and FHP, including unwanted extra invariants

**What the sources say:**

HPP (Hardy-Pomeau-de Pazzis, square D2Q4 lattice, 4 velocities, collision rule shown in Chopard's slide 9): "Conservation of mass and momentum." (Chopard PDF, slide 9). This is the physically intended invariant set.

But per Chopard's slide 13, titled "Spurious invariants," HPP also conserves:
- momentum along each line
- momentum along each column
- a checkerboard invariant

Search-result synthesis (Wikipedia lattice gas automaton page + secondary discussion found via search, not independently opened as a paper) elaborates on the checkerboard invariant: color the lattice like a checkerboard by parity of (x+y); a particle's trajectory alternates between one color class and the other every step, so particles starting on differently-colored sites never collide with each other — the automaton universe splits into two non-interacting subsystems. This is described as conservation of "total transverse momentum on even and odd lines... every two time-steps," and initial conditions must be chosen so both are zero or the artifact shows up in the flow.

Wikipedia (HPP model page) frames the same fact from the fluid-dynamics side: "momentum is always conserved in both the horizontal and vertical lanes" — described there as a flaw, together with the separate, distinct problem that HPP is not rotationally invariant (square-lattice anisotropy, square vortices) and does not dissipate energy (no true irreversible relaxation at the coarse level).

FHP (Frisch, Hasslacher, Pomeau 1986, hexagonal lattice, 6-7 velocities): per Chopard's slides, this was built specifically to fix the anisotropy problem via lattice symmetry ("the FHP model was introduced... as the first lattice gas automaton reproducing almost correct hydrodynamic behavior," i.e. converging to Navier-Stokes). Its stated conserved quantities are again mass and momentum (Chopard slide 10: "Stochastic rule with Conservation of mass and momentum"). I did not find, in the sources opened, an explicit statement that FHP eliminates all of HPP's spurious invariants (the hexagonal symmetry kills the line/column decomposition that produces the checkerboard trap on a square lattice, but I could not verify from a primary source whether FHP has its own residual spurious invariants). **Flag: unverified** whether FHP is fully clean of spurious invariants — Chopard's slides only assert the improvement in isotropy/hydrodynamic behavior, not a proof of zero extra invariants.

**How this would show up experimentally (my inference, not directly stated as such in sources):** an agent probing an HPP-like world with RUN/POKE would see globally conserved ball-count and momentum as expected, but on repeated trials would also notice that momentum summed over any single row, or any single column, stays constant over two-step intervals — an extra "law" that has no analog in real gases and that a naive single-global-momentum theory would miss (it would predict momentum exchange between rows that never actually happens). The checkerboard split (two independent interleaved sub-universes) would show up as: interventions poking cells of one parity class never affect the other parity class's future, even indirectly — a discoverable but easily-missed regularity, and a good "trap" for an agent that assumes a single connected dynamical system.

## 2. Where "balls fly straight" breaks — controlling parameter and regimes

**What the sources say directly:** Chopard's slide 6 states "if Ωi = 0, we obtain a free particle motion" — i.e. the collision operator is exactly what turns straight-line ballistic motion into something else; free streaming is the Ωi=0 limit of the same microdynamic equation used everywhere else, not a separate regime with different rules.

The later slides (around "Knudsen number expansion," slide ~24 of the PDF) frame the connection to hydrodynamics as a Chapman-Enskog-style expansion in a small parameter, explicitly called out as "a Knudsen number expansion" (∆t small compared to the typical variation time of the populations Ni). This matches the general lattice-gas/lattice-Boltzmann literature: the hydrodynamic (Navier-Stokes-like) description is recovered only in the limit of small Knudsen number Kn = λ/ℓ (mean free path over macroscopic length scale) — found via search summaries citing the standard Chapman-Enskog result used across LGCA/LBM literature (e.g. general LBM/Scholarpedia-style framing), which I treat as well-established textbook fact rather than a single-paper claim, and flag as **secondary-source corroborated, not from a single primary paper opened here**.

**My inference for the benchmark's "floors" language, grounded in the above:** the controlling parameter is occupation density (how full the ≤4-balls-per-cell lattice is), which directly sets the effective mean free path / collision frequency:
- Floor 1 ("balls fly straight"): sparse enough that Ωi ≈ 0 almost always — collisions rare, ballistic motion dominates, mean free path ≫ any length scale the agent probes at.
- Floor 2 ("collision rule needed"): density high enough that collisions are frequent but the population is still tracked exactly (Boltzmann/kinetic regime, not yet hydrodynamic) — this is the regime the HPP/FHP collision rule itself lives in.
- A further, hydrodynamic regime (implied by the Knudsen-number expansion in the sources but not one of the three named floors) is the coarse-grained continuum limit, small Kn, where individual collisions wash out and only conserved densities (mass, momentum) evolve smoothly — this is a distinct fourth regime from the three floors described in `_brief2.md`, worth flagging as available depth beyond Floor 3 if wanted.

I did not find in the sources opened an explicit named trichotomy "free-streaming / collisional / hydrodynamic regime" as a canonical taxonomy — this is my synthesis from (a) the Ωi=0 free-streaming statement and (b) the Knudsen-number hydrodynamic-limit statement, both directly sourced, bridged by ordinary kinetic-theory reasoning. **Flag: the middle "collisional but non-hydrodynamic" regime label is my inference, not a quoted term from these sources.**

## 3. Precedent for balls carrying internal state (colour/species) that only matters in collisions

**What the sources say:** The immiscible lattice gas of Rothman and Keller (1988) is the clearest precedent (found via search of arXiv comp-gas/9507001 "A Lattice-Gas Model of Microemulsions" and related abstracts — **these abstracts were read via search snippets, not fully fetched**, so treat the specifics below as secondary/summary-level, flagged accordingly):

- Particles carry a colour label (e.g. red/blue, ±1) in addition to the standard FHP velocity/occupation state.
- Colour does not change the mass/momentum conservation rules — those are still enforced exactly as in FHP.
- Colour changes *which* of the mass/momentum-conserving collision outputs is chosen: the collision rule is redefined to minimize the "work done by the colour flux against the local colour field," which biases outgoing colour-momentum toward like-colour regions. Net effect: colour segregation / interfacial tension / immiscibility emerge from a purely local, momentum-conserving rule — no new global conservation law is introduced for colour itself (colour is transported/relabeled by streaming and collision choice, not created or destroyed, but nothing in the summaries suggests colour density itself is a strictly conserved global quantity the way mass and momentum are — it is advected and locally redistributed by the biased collision choice).

**My inference relevant to Floor 3 (hidden inner state that flips on certain collisions):** this is a real physics precedent for the general pattern "internal label along for the ride, invisible in free streaming, only exercised at collision time, and it reshapes which of several momentum-conserving outcomes gets picked" — but the Rothman-Keller colour is *always* visible in principle (it's a physical property of the fluid, revealed by tracking any particle), whereas the benchmark's Floor 3 wants a colour that flips and is *only* detectable via designed crashes. I found no source describing a colour/internal-state variant that is deliberately hidden from direct observation and must be inferred purely from collision outcomes — that specific design (state as latent variable inferred only through crash tests) appears to be **novel relative to what these sources describe**, not documented lattice-gas practice. This is worth stating plainly as a design contribution rather than implying prior art we didn't find.

## 4. Collision rules that keep the system deterministic and exactly reversible; does reversibility give the experimenter an extra discoverable law

**What the sources say:**

- Chopard's slide 12 lists as a demo topic: "Exact calculation and time reversibility" for both HPP and FHP — i.e. reversibility is treated as a standard, checkable property of these models, not an incidental one.
- HPP's own collision rule (mass+momentum conserving on 4 velocities) is described in secondary/search summary as "the only nontrivial" deterministic collision law with those conservation properties on that lattice (found via search snippet referencing Wolfram/complex-systems material on Lattice Gas Hydrodynamics in Two and Three Dimensions — **not independently opened in full**, flagged as secondary).
- FHP's collision rule is explicitly stochastic where the mass/momentum constraints leave a tie: per search-summarized secondary sources, "some input situations produce two possible outcomes, and when this happens, one of them is picked at random" (e.g. head-on pair rotating 60° clockwise or counterclockwise with equal probability). This means canonical FHP as usually presented is *not* deterministic, and a probabilistic tie-break is standard practice, not an exotic hack. **Flag: this detail is from search snippets summarizing secondary sources (e.g. Wikipedia/course material describing FHP-I/II/III), not from opening the Frisch-Hasslacher-Pomeau 1986 paper itself.**
- A genuinely deterministic and *exactly, mechanically* reversible lattice gas is demonstrated directly in [arXiv cond-mat/0702074v2, "Entropy and Chaos in a Reversible Lattice Gas Cellular Automaton"](https://arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0702074v2) (D2Q9 automaton). That paper's construction: the collision look-up table must satisfy, for every column, the algebraic condition s = I·C·I·C(s), where I inverts all velocities and C is the collision map — i.e. "invert, collide, invert, collide" is the identity. This is a checkable, exact criterion for building a reversible deterministic collision rule (it is not restricted to HPP/FHP's specific tables but is a general recipe). The paper also uses quenched randomness (a fixed random integer assigned to each site at initialization, used only to pick among degenerate output columns) — reversibility is preserved because the randomness is quenched (fixed once, not resampled every step), not because the dynamics is deterministic in the naive sense.
- Experimentally in that paper: velocities are inverted at t=500 mid-run, and the system's entropy and Lyapunov expansion factor are shown to retrace back to their initial values by t=1000, then start relaxing toward equilibrium again — a direct, literal demonstration that "running the tape backward" via a full velocity-flip recovers the past exactly, with no Loschmidt paradox because the dynamics is genuinely microscopically reversible and deterministic (not an ensemble/Boltzmann-equation approximation).

**Extra discoverable law from reversibility (my inference, grounded in the paper's own framing):** yes — for an agent doing science on such a world, exact reversibility is itself a testable, falsifiable regularity distinct from mass/momentum conservation: "if I invert every ball's velocity right now, the world will retrace its exact prior history and then diverge again from that historical fixed point," including the counterintuitive prediction that macroscopic entropy will *decrease* for a while (the paper's own entropy curve literally reverses direction post-flip before rising again). This is a strong, order-of-magnitude weirder prediction than conservation laws, and cheap to test with POKE (flip all velocities, then compare against a saved earlier RUN trace). A world designer wanting an extra "gotcha" layer of discoverable law could use this directly, provided the world's collision rule is built to the I·C·I·C(s)=s criterion.

## 5. Known "traps": different-looking rules with identical observable behaviour

**What the sources say, and my synthesis:**

- The checkerboard/parity-split invariant of HPP (section 1) is itself a trap in the sense the brief cares about: two lattice-gas implementations that differ in how they handle same-parity vs. cross-parity coupling would look behaviorally identical under most "typical" random initial conditions (where both parity classes carry near-equal momentum and mass by symmetry) but would diverge sharply and reveal themselves as different programs the moment an agent POKEs an asymmetric, single-parity-class perturbation. **This is directly evidenced** by Chopard's explicit warning (slide 13 context) that initial conditions must be chosen so both odd- and even-line transverse momenta are zero, precisely because the spurious invariant is otherwise invisible in generic conditions but very much present and behaviorally consequential under special ones. That is a genuine, source-grounded example of "correct in typical behaviour but detectably different under a designed intervention" — good grading-design ammunition (design held-out test situations that break parity symmetry to unmask a program that got the collision rule structurally wrong but happens to pass symmetric tests).

- The FHP tie-breaking randomness (section 4) is a second, more direct trap candidate: **my inference**, not stated in these sources as a "trap" per se — if a submitted program resolves degenerate mass/momentum-conserving collisions (e.g. the two 60°-rotation choices) with a different random seed, different RNG algorithm, or even a different deterministic tie-break (e.g. "always rotate clockwise" instead of a coin flip), the two programs will be pointwise-different in most individual collision events yet statistically indistinguishable in bulk observables (density, momentum fields, macroscopic flow) over any single run, and only distinguishable if the grader checks frame-by-frame exact match on situations engineered to have few, isolated degenerate collisions (small numbers, so the specific tie-break outcome is directly visible rather than averaged away). This matches the brief's own grading philosophy (frame-by-frame exact match, not just statistical resemblance) and is worth flagging explicitly: **exact reproducibility of tie-break choices is a place where "correct physics, wrong code" and "correct code, wrong physics" can both masquerade as passing**, unless the benchmark either (a) makes the true world's tie-break fully deterministic (no randomness to reproduce), or (b) exposes the world's own random stream/seed to the agent as part of the discoverable rules, or (c) only grades bulk statistics where tie-break identity doesn't matter. I did not find this exact concern raised in the literature — it is my inference from combining the sourced facts about FHP degeneracy with the brief's own exact-match grading rule, so it should be read as a design warning, not a documented finding.

- No source opened here documents an actual pair of published lattice-gas rules that are provably behaviorally identical while structurally different (beyond the parity/checkerboard case, which is a single rule with a hidden splitting, not two different rules). **Flag: I found no direct primary-source example of "two genuinely different collision codes, indistinguishable in all tested behaviour"** — the closest documented analogues are (a) the checkerboard sub-universe split within one rule, and (b) FHP's own built-in degenerate-outcome randomness. Anything beyond that is inference above.

## Design recommendations (traceable to sources above)

1. **Use a hexagonal/FHP-style lattice, not a square/HPP lattice, for any floor meant to look isotropic and to avoid gifting the agent an easy discovered flaw** (source: Chopard slides 8-10; Wikipedia HPP page on square-lattice anisotropy/square vortices). If Floor 1/2 is deliberately built on a square lattice for simplicity, budget for the checkerboard/line-momentum spurious invariant becoming part of the discoverable truth (or explicitly design initial conditions to suppress it, per Chopard's own stated practice) — decide explicitly which case you're in, since it changes what "the correct theory" is.

2. **If any floor's true generator uses a degenerate, randomly-tie-broken collision rule (FHP-style), decide now whether the grader compares exact frames or bulk statistics.** Source-grounded finding: canonical FHP already relies on 50/50 random tie-breaks for symmetric collisions, so exact frame-matching over any run containing such collisions is either impossible without giving the agent the seed, or only fair if graded on distributions. Recommend either eliminating collision degeneracy in the true generator (choose a deterministic-but-not-obviously-biased tie-break) or explicitly deciding that held-out grading situations are chosen dense enough that individual random tie-breaks average out into the same macroscopic frame regardless of seed.

3. **Reversibility is a good, cheap, high-signal Floor 2/3 "gotcha" to add deliberately**, following the I·C·I·C(s)=s recipe from arXiv cond-mat/0702074v2: build the collision table so that inverting all velocities and re-running exactly retraces history. Then a designed POKE test ("flip everyone's velocity, run forward, does it retrace the earlier RUN?") becomes a strong discoverable law an agent can find and a submitted program must reproduce exactly, including the counterintuitive local "entropy decrease" during the retrace — this is a real, cited, demonstrated phenomenon, not speculative.

4. **The Floor-3 hidden-state design (colour that flips on certain collisions, invisible except through designed crashes) has real physics precedent in kind (Rothman-Keller colour) but not in the specific "hidden, must be inferred purely from crash outcomes" form** — the immiscible lattice gas's colour is always overtly present in the state. Recommend treating Floor 3 explicitly as a novel extension of the colour/species idea rather than implying it's drawn directly from lattice-gas literature; the collision-bias mechanism (colour reshaping which momentum-conserving outcome is chosen, without adding a new global conservation law) is a good template for how the hidden inner state should mechanically act.

5. **Strongest warning:** for any square-lattice (HPP-style) floor, do not test only with generic/symmetric initial conditions — per Chopard's explicit caveat, the spurious parity invariant is invisible under generic symmetric conditions and only shows up under special (odd-vs-even-line-imbalanced) ones. If held-out grading situations are all "generic," a structurally wrong submitted theory that ignores the parity split can still pass every test. Held-out sets should deliberately include parity-asymmetric interventions.
